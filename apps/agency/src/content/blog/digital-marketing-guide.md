I build websites for a living, and for years my marketing plan was "build a nice website and hope." Hope, it turns out, has terrible conversion rates.

So I went back to school — well, a course — and turned everything into this guide. It's the playbook I now run for every client, written so simply my nephew could follow it. This is a living post: I'm still working through the course, so new sections land here as I finish them.

> **TL;DR:** Good marketing is a fixed order of steps. Research who your buyers are before you say anything. Build the website around those people. Make Google like it (SEO). Collect emails and actually use them. Measure everything in Google Analytics. Then scale with influencers — and hold it all together with a brand.

## The Direct Answer

<div class="direct-answer">

A digital marketing guide is a step-by-step plan for getting customers online. The order matters: **research your audience first**, then build the website, then drive traffic (SEO, email, influencers), then measure and repeat. Skipping the research step is why most marketing money evaporates.

1. **Market research** — find out exactly who buys and why
2. **Website** — build it around those people
3. **SEO** — get found on Google
4. **Email** — capture visitors and bring them back
5. **Analytics** — see what actually works
6. **Influencers** — borrow someone else's audience
7. **Branding** — the glue that makes people remember you

</div>

Here's each step, kept short on purpose — with the one thing I do for every client.

## Step 1: Strategy — research before you touch anything

Everything starts with research, because guessing is expensive. You wouldn't sell skateboards to grandmas (well, most grandmas). Research tells you who wants your thing, what problem it solves for them, and what words make them care. Every step after this one leans on it.

**Per client:** no design, no copy, no ads until we know exactly who we're talking to.

## Step 2: Market research — find your people in three moves

This is simpler than it sounds. Three moves:

**Find your audience segments.** List the two or three distinct groups who need what you sell. For my agency that's local service businesses (roofers, HVAC, cleaners), solo founders launching something, and established businesses sitting on a site nobody has touched since flip phones. A quick trick: type "best [your product] for" into Google and let autosuggest finish the sentence — Google tells you who's searching.

**Verify they're real.** Check Google Trends and look for actual businesses or people matching each group. If a segment only exists in your head, cut it.

**Write one key message per segment.** Picture their situation, name their biggest problem, and say in one plain line how you fix it. A roofer doesn't want "responsive web solutions" — they want "your phone rings more."

**Per client:** define 2–3 buyer segments, verify they exist, and write one problem-solving message for each before anything else gets built.

## Step 3: Build the website — the home base you own

Social accounts rent you an audience; a website is the one channel nobody's algorithm can take away. It needs a clear hero section (who you are, what you do, why it matters), proof (reviews, real work), one section speaking to each audience segment from Step 2, and a fast, mobile-first build — because roughly three out of four visitors are on a phone.

This one's my day job, so I'll spare you the lecture — the full breakdown lives in [my four-step web design process](/blog/web-design-process). And if the site already exists but crawls, [here's why it's slow](/blog/why-is-my-website-slow).

**Per client:** one page section per audience segment, using that segment's key message — not one generic pitch for everyone.

## Step 4: SEO — make Google send you customers

SEO is four jobs, in order:

**Keyword research.** Find the words your buyers actually type. Longer phrases (four-ish words) are easier to win — "best hiking water bottle" beats fighting the whole internet for "water bottle". You want real search volume with low difficulty.

**SEO-friendly content.** One page, one keyword. Answer the question the searcher is really asking, in enough depth that they stop searching.

**On-page SEO.** The checklist: keyword in the title and first paragraph, a meta description that sells the click, clean headings, alt text on images, internal links between related pages.

**Backlinks.** Other relevant sites linking to you = votes of confidence. Quality over quantity — one on-topic link from a respected site beats fifty from junk directories.

**Per client:** pull their buyers' keywords, map one keyword per page, run the on-page checklist, then chase a handful of relevant local links.

## Step 5: Email marketing — the list you actually own

Email is the highest-leverage channel most small businesses ignore. Three jobs: set up a verified sending domain so mail lands in the inbox instead of spam, capture visitor emails with a simple form or a small incentive, and send campaigns using the per-segment messages from Step 2. I use [Resend](https://resend.com) for this — the pricing embarrasses the big-name platforms, and I've written up [the cost math here](/blog/amazon-ses-vs-resend).

**Per client:** verified sending domain, a lead-capture form on the site, and campaigns written per segment — never one blast to everybody.

## Step 6: Google Analytics — stop guessing, start looking

[Google Analytics](https://analytics.google.com) tells you where visitors come from, which pages they care about, and which campaigns turn into money. The whole principle in one line: do more of what the data says works, and quietly stop doing what doesn't.

**Per client:** install GA4, track leads and campaigns, filter out our own visits, and review the reports monthly.

## Step 7: Influencer marketing — borrow an audience

The highest-ROI channel in the course, and the least used because people think it means paying celebrities. It doesn't. The win comes from small, niche creators: a 5,000-follower account where 15% of people engage beats a 50,000-follower account where nobody does. Engagement rate matters more than follower count.

### The influencer triangle

Three players, and each one has to win or the deal dies. The **business** has a product and needs an audience. The **influencer** has an audience and needs money. The **audience** has money and needs a reason to buy — usually a discount. Your only real job is matching the right influencer to your buyer: the product must fit their audience.

### Where the influencers are

Mostly one place: **Instagram, home to about 81% of influencers.** YouTube (~21%), TikTok (~17%), and Facebook (~15%) trail far behind. Unless your niche screams video or a specific platform, start on Instagram.

### How to find and vet one, fast

1. **Search your niche hashtag** on Instagram (e.g. #plantbasedskincare) and browse who posts.
2. **Check the profile:** enough followers? right location (matters for local)? does their content fit your niche and your branding?
3. **Peek at their sponsored posts** — good ones feel like a friend's recommendation, not a TV commercial.
4. **Check the engagement rate** with a free tool like [HypeAuditor's Instagram engagement calculator](https://hypeauditor.com/free-tools/instagram-engagement-calculator/). Compare against the average for their size — a small account beating its average is the real prize.

### What influencers actually want

Money is not first. Surveyed reasons they work with businesses: **meaning** — shared values — leads at 61%, then **authority** (44%), **money** (~42%), and **free products** (~30%). A small brand with a story can out-recruit a big budget.

### The five tiers and what they cost

- **Nano** (1k–10k followers): $10–100 per post
- **Micro** (10k–50k): $100–500
- **Mid-tier** (50k–500k): $500–5,000
- **Macro** (500k–1M): $5,000–10,000
- **Mega** (1M+): $10,000 and up — into the millions for celebrities

Start at the bottom. Nano and micro creators are cheap, have the best engagement, and many happily work for free product.

### No budget? Go affiliate

Three steps: pick an affiliate platform, hand the influencer a personal link or discount code, and pay them a cut per sale they actually generate. Zero upfront risk — they only earn when you do.

### The whole campaign in five steps

1. **Set a budget** — decides everything after it
2. **Find influencers** — match the tier to the budget
3. **Reach out** — short, personal message about *their* work
4. **Sign** — deliverables, deadline, and tags agreed in writing
5. **Track and measure** — tracked link or code per influencer, aim for at least $2 back per $1 spent

**Per client:** shortlist small local or niche creators, rank by engagement rate, attach a tracked code to each, and report the ROI.

## Step 8: Branding vs marketing — the what and the how

Simplest way I've heard it: branding is **what** people feel and remember about you; marketing is **how** you reach them. Branding is your look, your values, what makes you different. Marketing is SEO, email, ads, social — the delivery trucks. Branding comes first and barely changes; marketing tactics change constantly. You need both: great marketing for a forgettable brand fills a leaky bucket, and a great brand nobody markets is a secret.

**Per client:** lock the brand basics — who you are, who it's for, what makes you different — before spending a cent on channels.

## Straight answers

**What is digital marketing in simple words?**
Getting customers through the internet — your website, Google, email, and social media — instead of billboards and cold calls. The core skill is knowing exactly who your buyer is and putting the right message where they already look.

**What should a small business do first in digital marketing?**
Market research. Define 2–3 groups of people who need what you sell, confirm they're real, and write one plain-language message for each. Every later step — website, SEO, email — reuses that work.

**What comes first, branding or marketing?**
Branding. It's what people remember about you; marketing is how you reach them. Set the brand once — who you are, who it's for, what makes you different — then point every marketing channel at it.

**Is influencer marketing worth it for small businesses?**
Yes, if you stay small. Niche creators with a few thousand engaged followers often promote for free product, and their audience actually listens. Pick by engagement rate, not follower count, and track every collaboration with its own discount code.

**How do I know if my marketing is working?**
Google Analytics. Track where visitors come from, which pages they engage with, and which campaigns produce leads or sales. If you can't point at a number, you're guessing.
