Start here

The 5 basics behind everything

Learn these five ideas first. Every other part of the system uses them.

1. Bidding

Your bid is the most you'll pay for one click. Every search runs a tiny auction. Google ranks ads by bid and quality, so the highest bidder doesn't always win, and you usually pay less than your maximum.

Manual vs. automatic

  • Manual: you set the price, like "up to $5 a click."
  • Smart bidding: you set a goal and Google sets the bid for every search. Examples: Maximize Clicks, Maximize Conversions, Target CPA.
  • Smart bidding learns from your tracking. Bad tracking teaches it the wrong thing.

2. Cost per click (CPC)

For Search ads, showing the ad is free. You pay only when someone clicks. Cost ÷ clicks = cost per click.

Why super-cheap clicks are a trap

Clicks at a few cents usually come from banner ads in apps and games, YouTube, Performance Max, or bots, not from people searching for a service. Always check the Conversions column, not the Clicks column.

3. Cost per acquisition (CPA)

How much money you spent to get one customer or one lead. In Google Ads: $300 spent ÷ 10 calls = $30 CPA.

Lower CPA is better. If a customer pays the business $200, a $30 CPA is a great deal. If a customer only pays $20, it loses money. Your job is to push this number down.

4. The "$30 a day" math

At $5 a click, $30 buys about 6 clicks a day, and many days bring zero leads. Over a month, that adds up to about 180 clicks and roughly 9–18 leads at 5–10%.

The honest pitch

"Give it 60–90 days. Judge it by cost per lead and booked jobs, not daily clicks." A better landing page or stronger negative keywords can double the leads for the same money.

01

Onboarding

Learn the business, then do the math

Before you sign a client, find out how much they can afford to pay for one lead. This number sets expectations from day one.

Questions to ask

  • Money: average job value, lifetime value, close rate, margin.
  • Capacity: how many new jobs a week can they handle?
  • Services: top 3 money-makers, and what they don't do (your first negative keywords).
  • Area: exact ZIP codes they serve, and ones they refuse.
  • Leads: who answers the phone, how fast, which CRM.
  • Goals: monthly ad budget and what "success in 90 days" means.

Ownership rules

  • The client creates and owns the Google Ads account and billing.
  • They link it to your manager account and give you Standard access.
  • Same for GA4, Tag Manager and Business Profile: you're a manager, not the owner.
  • Put it in the contract. This avoids fights if they ever leave.
02

The real first step

Tracking goes in before a single dollar

If Google can't see which clicks became calls, smart bidding is guessing. Nothing launches until every box is green.

Landing pages beat the homepage

  • One page per main service, e.g. "Termite Treatment in Jacksonville".
  • Service + city in the big headline. Tap-to-call button at the top on phones.
  • Short form: name, phone, ZIP, service.
  • Reviews, license and insurance, real photos, service area list.
  • Tracking number swapped in, and a hidden field that saves the Google click ID.
Your advantage: build one landing page template per industry and reuse it for every client.

Taking over an existing account?

Audit first: who owns it, what counts as a conversion, location settings, wasted search terms, and whether AI Max was switched on automatically.

03

Running the campaigns

Group by service, target only the real area

Organize Search campaigns into a few themes, one per service, each with its own landing page. Show ads only to people who are actually in the service area.

Themes, not single keywords

Old advice was one keyword per ad group. Today, experts group keywords that share the same intent, message and page. Aim for about 3–10 ad groups per campaign. Too many splits and Google never gets enough data.

Match types

  • Small new accounts: phrase and exact match on high-intent keywords.
  • Broad match only with smart bidding, strong negatives, and about 30 conversions a month.
  • AI Max replaced old broad-match campaigns in September 2026. Check inherited accounts and test it with a 50/50 experiment.

Schedule, devices, budget

  • Run ads when someone can answer the phone. Missed calls waste paid clicks.
  • Most local searches are on phones, so the page needs a sticky call button.
  • Rough floors: $1,000–$1,500/month for handyman or barber, $1,500–$3,000+ for pest control. Focus on one or two services.
04

Ads & assets

Make the ad bigger than the competition

An ad is a headline and a description, plus assets: extra rows like call buttons, links and callouts. More assets mean a bigger ad and more taps.

Responsive Search Ads

  • 1–2 ads per ad group, using all 15 headlines and 4 descriptions.
  • Mix: service + city, an offer, trust, speed, and a call to action.
  • Pin as little as possible. Judge ads on leads, not on "ad strength".

Assets to set up every time

  • Call with the tracking number, during business hours.
  • Location from the Business Profile.
  • Sitelinks (4–8), callouts, structured snippets, real images, logo.
  • Price or promotion assets for barbers and restaurants.
Count a call only after about 60 seconds. Short calls are often wrong numbers or spam.
05

Bidding ladder

Climb one step at a time

Smart bidding needs data. Each step up the ladder needs more conversions in the last 30 days.

1. Launch: Maximize Clicks

Set a max cost per click cap. It builds data cheaply while there are no conversions yet.

2–3. Maximize Conversions, then Target CPA

Switch once leads start coming in. Move to Target CPA after about 30 conversions in 30 days, and start the target near the real recent CPA.

Learning period rules

  • Don't judge a change in the first 1–2 weeks.
  • Move targets by 15–20% at a time, 7–10 days apart.
  • Small local accounts rarely reach 30 conversions, which is another reason to keep campaigns few.
06

Tracking the data

Teach Google which leads became jobs

Counting form fills isn't enough. The best agencies send booked and sold jobs back to Google, so it hunts for real customers, not tire-kickers.

Count these (Primary)

  • Calls from ads, about 60 seconds or longer.
  • Calls from the website through the tracking number.
  • Form submissions and completed bookings.
  • Later: qualified lead or booked job imports.

Just watch these (Secondary)

Clicks on the phone number, GA4 engagement. For restaurants and barbers, directions can be Primary because that's the goal.

The tools

  • Tag Manager + GA4 on every page.
  • CallRail or WhatConverts: swaps the phone number per visitor so each call is tied to the ad.
  • Hidden form field for the click ID (GCLID) and UTM tags.
  • Client tags every lead in the CRM. You import the good ones.

Test before launch, and monthly

Submit a test form and make a test call from an ad click. Both must appear in Google Ads, GA4 and the CRM, with no double counting.

07

Excluding keywords

Negative keywords: the filter that saves money

A negative keyword tells Google "never show my ad for searches with this word". People searching "pest control jobs" want a job, not an exterminator.

The 3 negative match types

jobsBroad: blocks any search with all the words, any order. "bug spray"Phrase: blocks searches with those words in that order. [cheap exterminator]Exact: blocks only that exact search.

Negatives don't catch plurals or synonyms. Add "job" and "jobs", "career" and "careers" yourself.

Build them in layers

1Account-level "never" list (also protects Performance Max)
2Shared "Universal Local" list on every campaign
3Shared industry list: pest, handyman, barber, restaurant
4Out-of-area cities list
5Ad group negatives to route traffic to the right group

Starter negative lists

Pick a list, copy it, and paste it into Google Ads. Remove anything the client actually sells. Green words are buyers, so never block them.

Weeks 1–4

Check the search terms report every 2–3 days. New accounts leak the most money.

First 90 days

Weekly. Sort by cost, block bad patterns, look for new keyword ideas.

After that

Weekly with broad match or AI Max. Every two weeks for small exact/phrase accounts.

Golden rule: never add a negative from imagination. "Free estimate" and "price" often come from buyers. Start with the obvious list, then let real search terms guide you.

    2Cost per lead and cost per booked job

    4Return: revenue ÷ (ad spend + your fee)

    Scaling your agency

    Pricing, templates, and traps to avoid

    Build everything once as a template, then reuse it for every client. That's how a small agency handles many accounts.

    Flat monthly fee

    ~$500–$2,000

    Most common: about 83% of PPC firms in one survey. Define the deliverables to avoid scope creep.

    % of ad spend

    10–20%

    Usually with a minimum fee. You earn more when they spend more, which some clients distrust.

    Setup fee

    $500–$2,500

    One time, for tracking, landing page and account build. Your web work can be priced separately.

    RECOMMENDED FOR YOU

    Setup fee + flat monthly fee ($500–$1,000 for budgets under ~$5k). Switch to a percentage above ~$10k/month in spend. Require at least $1,000–$1,500/month in ad budget (pest control $2,000+). Start with a 3-month term, because the first 60–90 days build the data.

    The 11 most common mistakes

    1. Creating the client's ad account inside your manager account.
    2. Launching before tracking works, or counting every click as a lead.
    3. Leaving location on "Presence or interest".
    4. Broad match, AI Max or Performance Max on tiny budgets with no negatives.
    5. Sending every ad to the homepage.
    6. Too many campaigns, so none reach 30 conversions.
    7. Blocking "free" when the client offers free estimates.
    8. Never importing booked jobs, so Google optimizes for junk forms.
    9. Ignoring missed calls and after-hours handling.
    10. Not exporting LSA history before the move.
    11. Accepting Google's recommendations blindly.
